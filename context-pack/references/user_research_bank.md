| **1. Learning how to practise** - A review of 17 studies examined teaching learners to plan practice, evaluate attempts and change strategy. In one 28-student study, practice-technique videos plus goal-setting and self-evaluation instruction produced better performance than technique videos alone. However, a five-week study of 234 students found no significant performance advantage from self-evaluation instruction. The review therefore provides examples of useful teaching mechanisms, with mixed results - not one established effect across learners. | [Utermohl de Queiroz et al. - Pedagogical approaches to promote self-regulation in musical instrument learning](https://doi.org/10.1177/03057356251348414). Full-text review; the individual studies are reported within it. | 24 Jul 2025 online; Sep 2026 issue. | A learner may know which note was wrong but still lack a way to improve it. The relevant skill is selecting and evaluating a practice strategy, beyond repeating the whole exercise. | **Core - a guided practice cycle** - I could guide the learner through one goal > an attempt > identifying one difficulty > choosing a strategy, such as slowing down or practising two beats > a retry. I would explain why the strategy fits the observed difficulty. |
| **2. Feedback focus and timing** - In a randomised violin study, 50 analysed beginners practised bowing with or without real-time visual feedback. The feedback group improved bow movement but could produce worse sound while following the display. In the immediate test after feedback removal, the clear statistically significant advantage was steadier volume; improvement was not established across every measure. The session lasted roughly 1-1.5 hours. | [Blanco, Tassani & Ramirez - Real-Time Sound and Motion Feedback for Violin Bow Technique Learning](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.648479). Full text. | 26 Apr 2021. | A display can redirect attention: success on the visual target and success in the sound are different outcomes. This gives me a specific feedback-design problem to test. | **Core - one feedback target at a time** - I could show one actionable observation after a phrase, such as the last two notes arriving early. I would include replay, a retry and a new attempt without the visual guide. I would test this translation from violin to the app. |
| **3. Learning to evaluate my own playing** - Sixteen college guitarists practised a new piece across ten sessions; eight watched recordings on four occasions and eight did not. Researchers analysed spoken self-evaluations. The detailed review reports that video users increasingly discussed strategies for subsequent practice, particularly stronger performers. The evidence concerns the content of their reflection, rather than proving that playback generally raises performance scores. | [Boucher, Creech & Dubé - Video feedback and the self-evaluation of college-level guitarists](https://doi.org/10.1177/0305735619842374). Original abstract checked; detailed extraction in the [full-text review](https://doi.org/10.1177/03057356251348414). | 26 Apr 2019 online; Mar 2021 issue. | Performing and judging a performance simultaneously can be difficult. Reviewing an attempt afterwards creates space to decide what to change next. | **Core - replay > learner observation > feedback** - I could ask the learner to identify a difficult section before revealing the app’s observation. For a virtual instrument, I would replay recorded note events, so I would not need video recording for this prototype. |
| **4. Why learners disengage** - A follow-up surveyed 104 former school instrument learners; 59 provided usable paired ratings of their most engaged period and the period before stopping. Belonging was lower, while felt pressure and perceived incompetence were higher before stopping. The positive autonomy and competence scales did not differ significantly. I need to distinguish between those measures when interpreting the findings. The comparison was retrospective. | [Evans, McPherson & Davidson - The role of psychological needs in ceasing music and music learning activities](https://doi.org/10.1177/0305735612441736). [Full text](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/2013_EvansMcPhersonDavidson_Roleof.pdf). | 9 May 2012 online; 2013 issue. | Difficulty is only part of disengagement. Feeling pressured, unable to improve or disconnected from other musicians can also matter. This study does not show which app intervention changes those experiences. | **Keep - learner choice and visible personal progress** - I could let students choose a musical goal and compare successive attempts. I would consider collaboration later; this evidence alone would not lead me to build a social network or retention mechanics. |
| **5. Different ways to participate in the same music lesson** - A review of 19 studies covering ages 6-15 identified practices such as alternative notation, adapted instruments and repertoire, movement/body percussion, and peer support. Some studies reported that alternative symbols helped students express musical ideas and compose with classmates. The studies used varied designs; these were context-dependent practices, not a single tested accessibility package. | [Mommo, Sutela & Mononen - Inclusion and pedagogical support for students with special educational needs in music lessons](https://doi.org/10.1177/1321103X251342143). Full text. | 11 Jun 2025. | The barrier may be the representation or action required to participate, rather than the musical concept itself. “Personalisation” can therefore involve changing how the same concept is presented. | **Keep a small set of options** - I could offer an audio demonstration, labelled rhythm blocks and adjustable tempo. I would test whether learners can work towards the same rhythm objective through these options, using observation and preference to guide my choices. |
| **6. Accessible instruments must also be practical to operate** - A participatory project involved 11 students, seven assistants and two music teachers. Prototypes used large touch surfaces, buttons, joysticks and pressure controls. A laptop-connected guitar failed to work as intended when the researcher was absent; teachers could subsequently use standalone instruments independently. This gives me a concrete implementation issue to account for. | [Förster & Schnell - Designing accessible digital musical instruments for special educational needs schools](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijcci.2024.100666). [Full text](https://d-nb.info/1340885417/34). | 13 Jul 2024 online. | A potentially expressive instrument is of little use if its controls or setup prevent participation. Who can start, configure and recover the tool matters alongside what it sounds like. | **Keep simple controls; deeper accessibility is an alternative project** - I could prototype large pads and keyboard/touch input, with settings that persist. I would test whether a learner or teacher can launch the activity unaided. I would develop specialised input hardware through direct co-design. |
| **7. Access without having to explain or disclose repeatedly** - Interviews with 18 Australian conservatoire students described fear of stigma or lowered expectations after disclosure, difficult schedules and environments, and frustration accessing support. Some valued online participation. Most participants reported non-apparent conditions; several disability groups were unrepresented. These accounts identify concrete barriers but do not test an interface intervention. | [Thompson et al. - Hidden diversity in the conservatoire](https://doi.org/10.1177/02557614241237235). Full text. | 8 Mar 2024 online; 2025 issue. | A learner may need a quieter display, a break or another way to access material without wanting to provide a diagnosis. These are participation requirements, not necessarily musical skill deficits. | **Keep user-controlled access settings** - I could include pause/resume, replayable instructions and controls for optional animation or sound effects. I would make these available without medical disclosure. I see these as usability decisions; the research does not establish an AI requirement here. |
| **8. Teachers may need help delivering the lesson** - The ACER survey received 706 responses from 95 NSW public primary schools. Among the relevant respondents, 55% reported under ten hours of initial music training and 82% felt insufficient time had been devoted to preparation for teaching through Year 6. Additionally, 73% preferred prerecorded lessons to leading their own; 75% wanted more expertise/training, 67% more resources and 61% more time. | [Taylor & Edwards / ACER - Primary Teachers’ Survey, NSW](https://research.acer.edu.au/tll_misc/49/). [Full report](https://music-education.squarespace.com/s/ACER-NSW-Survey-Full-Report.pdf). | 19 May 2025. | This points to a different potential user: the generalist teacher. A student app does not automatically solve uncertainty about demonstrating a concept, structuring practice or recognising a useful next step. | **Alternative MVP - a playable lesson assistant** - I could let a teacher select a musical objective and available time, then provide a demonstration, guided class activity and observation prompts. I would choose this direction if teacher interviews reveal a stronger need than the independent learner coach. |
| **9. Getting started can depend on money and location** - The NSW inquiry documented concerns about user-pays provision, expensive private tuition, uneven school opportunities and regional access. Its evidence comes from submissions, hearings and stakeholder accounts across arts and music education. It establishes a plausible access problem, not a finding that a digital lesson would overcome each barrier. | [NSW Parliament - Arts and Music Education and Training, Chapter 7](https://www.parliament.nsw.gov.au/lcdocs/inquiries/3048/Report%20No.%201%20-%20Joint%20Select%20Committee%20on%20Arts%20and%20Music%20Education%20and%20Training%20in%20New%20South%20Wales.pdf). Report chapter checked. | Dec 2024. | An app may help someone begin making and learning music before acquiring an instrument or regular tuition. Device access, human support and opportunities to play with others still need consideration. | **Core delivery choice - an instrument-free first lesson on an existing device** - I could use a playable virtual surface for the initial concept. I would treat costs, device requirements and connection needs as design constraints; I would not use this finding alone to justify AI. |
| **10. Existing software has already been used in supported music learning** - A six-week rural Australian outreach project used GarageBand with upper-primary and lower-secondary students experiencing school difficulties. The accessible abstract describes focus groups, observations and programme notes, and reports a positive impact without enough outcome detail to determine which activity produced it. Only the abstract was available for this review, so I would keep this as background. | [English, Lumb & Davidson - What are the affordances of the digital music space in alternative education?](https://doi.org/10.1177/0255761421999731). **Abstract only** | 10 Mar 2021. | The paper confirms an existing example of facilitated learning with GarageBand. It does not, from the accessible material, establish a specific missing capability my app should provide. | **Background / hold** - I would compare my prototype with what learners can already do using a playable instrument and ordinary guidance. I would demonstrate what happens after a learner struggles, alongside the playable instrument itself. |
