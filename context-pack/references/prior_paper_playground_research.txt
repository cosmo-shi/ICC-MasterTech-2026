    A place to make a little noise
    Design research and direction for a children's
    music-learning app




    8 November 2026 · Recommendation: Paper Playground

    The app should feel like a creative place children are invited to use: bold, warm, curious, a little cheeky,
    and generous about mistakes. Its personality should come from a recognisable visual language and
    responsive musical play.

    My recommendation is a paper-and-print-inspired world with sturdy expressive lettering, warm off-white
    space, vivid flat colour, a small family of sound shapes, and simple controls. Think of a children's creative
    museum or a hands-on print studio. The mood-board image is an original generated concept, not a
    finished app, an exact font specimen, or evidence of usability.

    The aim is childlike imagination with clear interaction. An all-ages brand can share a visual identity, but a
    six-year-old, a teenager and a parent will still need different reading levels, content and support.

    This review retained 115 source pages and relevant passages from 148 screened records. The remaining
    33 were blocked, empty, limited or excluded. These are page-level sources, not 115 independent studies.
    The companion register identifies every record and its status.

    1. The design decision
    Paper Playground is a direction name, not a proposed app name. It translates “playful, welcoming, free and
    childish” into five practical rules:

    1. Making is the main event. The first screen invites a sound or a small creative action. Children should hear
    the result of an action before confronting a progress dashboard.

    2. A bold silhouette does more than more decoration. Use a ring, a block, an arch and a wave as a small
    starting vocabulary. They can become sound tiles, illustration parts and motion cues.

    3. Personality has a home. Give headings, illustrations and transitions character. Keep directions, controls,
    labels and the instrument surface stable and readable.

    4. Mistakes are reversible. Replay, stop, undo and try-again controls should be obvious. In creative play, a
    different sound is not automatically an incorrect answer.

    5. The learner owns the sound. Saved creations and meaningful choices should be more prominent than
    trophies, streaks or a mascot's approval.

      Headings                              Gabarito, weight 800                      A sturdy, friendly voice without baby talk


      Reading and controls                  Figtree, weights 400-600                  A quieter layer that supports the action

    The recommendation is a design judgement informed by the sources. No study establishes that this
    particular aesthetic will improve music learning. It needs testing with the intended learners. [S016, S018,

    where recognising the real instrument is part of the learning goal. Abstract sound play and accurate
    instrument teaching need different visual treatments.

    6. Typography: a shortlist with a purpose
    Start with Gabarito 800 for headings and Figtree 400-600 for reading and controls. Gabarito's official
    description places friendliness and educational use within its design story. Figtree supplies a calmer
    supporting voice. This is a tone-and-function recommendation, not evidence that either family is the
    most readable for children. [S117, S048]

    Use expressive type for short headings, chapter names and selected celebratory moments. Keep
    instructional sentences, instrument labels, timings and error recovery in the reading family. Avoid forcing
    every letter to wobble, rotating functional labels or using novelty lettering for a whole paragraph.

    As prototype starting points, try approximately 20 CSS px for child-facing directions, 18-20 px for control
    labels, and 32-44 px for short display headings, with generous spacing and room to resize. These are
    design proposals, not WCAG-prescribed minimum font sizes. Test at the real device size and viewing
    distance.

    Andika is a useful beginning-reader alternative because its design deliberately differentiates potentially
    confusing characters. Atkinson Hyperlegible Next is another option worth testing, especially for character
    distinction. Neither removes the need for testing with different readers. [S049, S050]

    Check: I l 1 | O 0 | rn m | a g | Béa | 1, 2, 3. Check supported scripts, accents, translations, numerals and
    music symbols separately. A single Latin font does not establish international accessibility. Test long
    labels at 200% text size. Single-storey a/g forms are a contextual choice, not a universal rule. [S068,
    S085, S118, S120]

    7. What font evidence supports
    Size, spacing, character distinction and reader differences deserve more weight than “friendly font”
    marketing. Hughes and Wilkins' child-reading study found the younger participants were affected by
    decreasing text size. Wilkins and colleagues' later work also connects typographic format with
    performance in particular child-reading tasks. These are not direct tests of a modern music app. [S083,
    S084]

    Studies of specialist dyslexia fonts do not justify promising that a particular branded font will solve
    reading difficulty. Kuster and colleagues found no reading advantage for Dyslexie over the comparison
    fonts in their experiments. Preference and performance are different measures. [S086]

    Microsoft's Kermit project is interesting contemporary typography work, especially its approach to letter
    differentiation and animation. Its own write-up says the proposed reading benefit of an animated feature
    was still being tested. That is a good example of why a design rationale and a demonstrated outcome
    should be kept separate. [S119]

      Claim to avoid                                A more defensible statement


      “Rounded fonts are accessible”                Some rounded families suit the desired voice; reading depends on the actual
                                                    letterforms, size, spacing and reader


      “Sans serif always reads better”              Compare appropriate families in the real task; serif status alone is insufficient


      “A dyslexia font makes this inclusive”        Provide clear text, flexibility and testing; specialist-font claims need evidence


      “Children like bright colours”                Age, task, culture, individual needs and context affect preference and use


      “Neuro-friendly UI”                           State the tested needs and observed outcomes; a label or theory reference is
                                                    insufficient


    For typography testing, use the same instruction in two or three candidate families at comparable
    apparent sizes. Look at what children misread, how much help they need and whether they can locate an
    action. Ask which feels welcoming afterwards. Do not select a family from preference alone.

    8. Colour: the recommended working palette
    The palette's character comes from a warm neutral, a dark anchor and a small group of vivid accents.
    These colours are an authored proposal, not a scientific prescription for children's emotions.

      Name                                  Hex                              Proposed role


      Paper                                 #FFF8EC                          Main background and quiet space


      Midnight ink                          #252545                          Text, essential outlines and high-contrast
                                                                             controls


      Tomato                                #F26A4F                          Hero artwork, selected sound tiles and
                                                                             emphasis


      Sunflower                             #FFD45C                          Creative prompts, highlight surfaces and
                                                                             illustration


      Pool blue                             #87CFE5                          Secondary sound surfaces and illustration


      Deep teal                             #147D76                          Primary action and selected states


      Quiet text                            #5C5C73                          Secondary text on paper


    As an initial composition exercise, try roughly 60% paper, 25% vivid surfaces and 15% ink/deep anchors on
    a landing or discovery screen. This is a sketching heuristic, not an optimal ratio. Instrument screens should
    allocate space according to the task.

    Think in roles as well as hues. Ink anchors reading. Deep teal identifies the main action. Tomato, sunflower
    and pool blue create the world around that action. An exercise can use only two or three of these colours
    rather than displaying the whole palette at once.

    The same palette can be loud on a discovery illustration and quiet on a practice screen. That flexibility is
    more useful for longevity than adding a new fashionable colour every season. If the palette changes later,
    preserve meaningful action and state distinctions.

    9. Illustration, motion and the play surface
    Build a small grammar. Start with four silhouettes: a ring, a block, an arch and a wave. Decide how corners,
    outlines, facial features and texture behave. A short set of rules helps different screens feel related and
    makes the asset workload feasible. An abstract shape is not distinctive by itself; consistent use and
    memorable combinations can make it associated with the app over time. [S011, S013, S016]

    A percussion action might briefly compress its tile. A held note might extend a line. A musical phrase
    might grow a short sequence of marks. These are proposed behaviours to prototype. Avoid assigning
    supposedly universal colour-emotion meanings to timbres or teaching an inaccurate visual model of
    sound.

    Choose a small cast or even a single companion initially. The learner's instrument and creation should
    receive the attention. A character can demonstrate, react or invite another attempt. It should not express
    disappointment to pressure a return visit.

      Screen                                Emphasise                                    Keep quieter


      Discover                              A few clear invitations and intriguing       Analytics, rankings and long descriptive
                                            sounds                                       cards


      Play                                  The playable surface, response, stop and     Mascot movement and unrelated decoration
                                            replay


      Practise                              One instruction, relevant cue and specific   Multiple simultaneous goals and reward
                                            feedback                                     counters


      My sounds                             What the learner made and how to replay      Competitive comparisons

    Motion should explain cause and effect. Nonessential animation needs a reduced-motion path;
    concentration tasks should not be ringed by perpetual bouncing. In a music app, decoration should not
    introduce extra sounds while a learner is trying to listen. Keep sound effects separately controllable from
    instructional audio where practical. [S070-S072]

    Offer tap/select alternatives to dragging, keyboard operation and meaningful accessible labels. Start
    around 56 CSS px for major play targets where feasible, with generous separation; validate against motor
    needs and screen constraints. WCAG's 24 CSS px AA target criterion includes exceptions, and its
    enhanced 44 CSS px criterion is AAA. These are not claims that 24 px is an ideal child-sized control.
    [S065, S066, S069]

    10. All-ages appeal and accessibility
    A shared brand is realistic. One identical interface for all ages and abilities is a much weaker proposition.
    Child and teen usability research supports paying attention to developmental differences and avoiding
    adult stereotypes about what young users want. [S132, S133]

      User or situation                     Keep in the common identity                    Adapt in the experience


      Younger or beginning reader           Palette, silhouettes, core control positions   Less reading, optional narration, concrete
                                                                                           actions and adult support


      Older child or teenager               Creative tone and recognisable artwork         More agency, greater depth and less praise
                                                                                           for trivial actions


      Parent or teacher                     The same visual family                         Clear activity purpose, settings and useful
                                                                                           progress information in the appropriate area


      Low vision or reading                 Brand character and clear hierarchy            Text size, contrast, character distinction,
      differences                                                                          screen-reader structure and user
                                                                                           preferences


      Motor differences                     Predictable controls                           Larger targets, alternative inputs and no
                                                                                           drag-only route


      Sensory needs                         Optional visual personality                    Reduced motion, sound-effect controls,
                                                                                           pause and a calmer presentation


      Hearing differences                   Access to making and exploring                 Visual timing, available haptics and suitable
                                                                                           alternate goals; don't claim these reproduce
                                                                                           every listening task


    UNICEF's RITEC framework makes autonomy, identity, creativity, competence, safety and relationships
    relevant design questions. Apply that through control, representation and opportunities to make
    something personally meaningful, not through a “wellbeing” label on the interface. [S078, S079]

    Where the app includes regional instruments or cultural learning, use accurate names and representations,
    and give the relevant people authority over how material appears. Do not use generic “world music”
    decoration as a shortcut to inclusion. In the prototype, a smaller set of well-represented instruments is
    preferable to a large inconsistent catalogue.

    Learning and visual attractiveness are different outcomes. Learning-science work highlights active,
    engaged, meaningful and socially interactive experiences around a learning goal. Coherence research
    gives a reason to question appealing but irrelevant extras. The reviewed gamification meta-analysis also
    distinguishes motivation from competence-related outcomes. [S092, S095, S097]

    12. Validate the direction before polishing it
    Use a small formative round to find problems, then iterate. A practical first round might include four to six
    participants in each priority age group, plus caregivers or educators and participants with relevant access
    needs. That is a planning suggestion, not a sample size that can establish population-level learning
    effects. Use appropriate consent and assent.

    Compare Paper Playground with the quieter current direction using the same content and task. Keep task
    difficulty comparable and vary order where practical. Do not show a beautiful polished concept against an
    unfinished control and attribute the difference to the aesthetic alone.

    13. Brief to give a designer or coding assistant
    Design a children's music-learning app as a Paper Playground: a place to try, make and explore sound. The
    voice is curious, welcoming, cheeky, expressive and generous about mistakes. It should also be
    comfortable for older children and adults.

    Use Gabarito 800 for short headings and Figtree 400-600 for reading and controls. Preserve normal
    readable word shapes in directions. Consider Andika as a beginning-reader option and test character
    distinction, text resizing and target-language support.

    Use warm paper #FFF8EC, midnight ink #252545, tomato #F26A4F, sunflower #FFD45C, pool blue
    #87CFE5 and deep teal #147D76. Use ink text on the pale and bright surfaces; white can be used on deep
    teal. Check every state. Use colour with labels, shape or position, never as the only indicator.

    Build illustrations from a consistent ring, block, arch and wave vocabulary. Use occasional painted-paper
    or dry-print texture in artwork. Keep the playable surface and text clean. Develop a small original cast
    rather than borrowing a mascot. Every movement should respond to an action or clarify a musical event.
    Provide a calmer reduced-motion presentation.

    Make first sound, stop, replay, undo and saving a creation obvious. Prefer large, well-separated controls
    and a small number of meaningful choices. Add keyboard and non-drag alternatives. Keep progress
    information useful and place it where the relevant learner or adult needs it.

    Avoid generic assistant sparkles, frosted-glass controls, decorative gradients across all screens, excessive
    shadow cards, music-note wallpaper, baby talk, a copied lesson path and rewards attached to every
    action. A style is not prohibited in principle; each choice must serve this direction and the task.

    Show a discovery screen, a play screen and a recovery/try-again state at real device size. Include
    large-text and reduced-motion versions. Explain how the same rules connect the screens. Treat the mood
    board as a starting hypothesis and test it with intended learners before declaring it accessible or
    effective.
