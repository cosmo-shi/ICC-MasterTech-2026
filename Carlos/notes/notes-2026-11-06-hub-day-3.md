## Ivan's feedback
- Good that the problem came from research; now make it human-centred. You three have the problem; do others? What happens if unsolved?
- We each value it for a different reason: practise alone (Carlos, introvert), class too big and fear of mistakes (Justin), no piano at home (Simone). Find the common theme.
- Resource spectrum: voice (everyone has one), guitar (cheap), piano (expensive). Be specific about where the problem lives.
- Traditional music learning needs three things in one place: a place, an instrument, a teacher. Remove any one and the model breaks.
  o Where the instrument or teacher is missing, the app replaces; where present, it augments. Both, by context.
- Find the research: does missing music early reduce the chance of learning it later? Also the cultural cost to a country.
- Be specific about the learner and the stage: getting started, exploring, or wanting feedback without enough teacher time.
- Choosing an instrument is rarely deliberate; finding your passion is costly. Easy exploration is a problem worth solving.
- Feedback is where AI is a cheap substitute for educator time; phone camera plus microphone is a good use.
- Other emerging technologies: not required in the prototype; put the vision on a slide (AR, sound masking were floated).
- Pitch: paint the picture first (a student starting high school with no music class), then the statistic.

### What we told Frank (Simone led)
- Problem: music education is downsized from primary to high school; resources, access and finances.
  o Whether you learn music now depends on money and a nearby teacher, not curiosity or talent.
- Solution: a digitised way to learn fundamentals (piano and others), sheet music and notes at home, own pace.
  o AI gives personalised feedback on how to improve, tailored to the learner.
- Status: idea decided today; functional requirements next; a mini prototype today.
- Feedback relayed to Frank: hone the audience; find the root cause of the downsizing; do not oversell.
  o A phone is not a real instrument, so offer the theory path or straight to simulation.

### Frank's feedback
- Musician from a musical family; noticed the gap when he arrived in Australia ten years ago.
  o "Music is emotion"; the idea grabbed him at once; others will feel it too.
- Stakeholders are the sponsors; they must feel "this is something we can actually do something with".
  o Not only enrolled students: UC does outreach to regional and rural areas and pathways for non-ATAR students.
  o For a university: why would I implement this to support my students or my outreach?
- Trust: AI is over-hyped; expect "can I trust AI as a teacher" and "will AI replace me".
  o Offer basic lessons plus a short session with a real teacher, e.g. ten minutes reviewing your work.
  o "The future is about humans and AI working together"; this use case shows it.
- Personalisation for teachers: one lecturer teaches 250 students the same way; each learns differently.
  o AI lets a teacher personalise without the cost of one-to-one; music is a very good area to show it.
- "Teaching is a human business; music is highly emotional; show how AI amplifies and supports that."
  o People think AI plus music means generating background music; show AI in a different light.
- Simplicity: the biggest pitfall is stuffing too much into three minutes.
  o One key thing; end with a glimpse of future possibilities, "everything you haven't done".
- MasterTech is largely an integration exercise; only so much code fits; start designing, choose the technology.
- Carlos asked about students considering a music degree. Frank: less on enrolled students.
  o Universities offer it to their students and to schools through outreach, converting them into future students.
- Netherlands contrast: weekly music at school; singing is not scary there, somehow it is here.
  o Community choir members say "I should have known singing was so much fun much earlier" (anecdote).
- Frank's account of a renowned Canberra school of music: performance-focused, folded into a university to cut cost, academics pushed to publish, people left, cut again recently (his account; verify before use).
- Punchline ideas: "what is a society without music?"; Australia is one of the least diverse economies, "we buy everything from the northern hemisphere", apparently even musical talent (verify the economy claim).
  o Frank rates Simone's punchlines; she writes the first and last lines.
- Frank: 80% likely at the pitch on 11 Nov; a colleague who combines robotics and art may be worth a talk later.

### Stanley (Perth; daughter aged seven; classical guitar at a music school, then lead guitar from CDs and books)
- "It's quite a wide scope": start from a mock-up of how the app looks; do not worry about the back end yet.
- Primary schools have music only if they have a music teacher; not core; most start on the plastic recorder.
- Define the market first: an app for young adults will never work for a seven-year-old (graphics, pace, language, fonts, sizes).
- His own gap: after lessons, CDs and books gave "no feedback. That's the problem with those courses."
- Before Monday nail down: the target age group, beginner onwards, and the instruments (two at most: piano plus one).
- Lowest-hanging fruit is keyboard: feedback on a tablet works; guitar needs a mic or MIDI box, "accessibility is not there"; kids start on keyboard.
- Judges are Cisco plus the three sponsor universities; aim the presentation at the panel; if it clicks with a judge you will see it.
- Need a hook in the demo that is not generic; first job: survey every music app and show what is missing and where the AI feedback comes in.

### Chris (plays guitar; son plays guitar, drums, trumpet)
- Be careful what else is out there; how do you differentiate; what is the hook that is not there now? Agreed that "videos cannot see how you play" is the hook.
- Pitch like pitching to an investor: who would pay, the university or people directly, and what do they want to hear? Hit that audience's care-abouts.
- "Very cool idea, very different, I like it."
- For the demo, one instrument (guitar or piano); put the vision of expanding to a whole orchestra in the presentation. "You could bring one of your guitars."

### Third advisor (arrived mid-pitch; background in skill acquisition; cannot play an instrument; probably Madison)
- Asked how input works: keys on the phone, or audio? Justin: piano layout on the phone; Carlos: audio for voice.
- Liked feedback in layman's terms: in skill acquisition the feedback must be prescriptive and detailed, yet human instructors often cannot articulate it simply. "Get that portion right."
- Show visually what the learner should have done instead; mention learning styles (visual, auditory) as a future expansion.
- Dynamic adjustment is essential: enough challenge to make them problem-solve, not so much they are overwhelmed; people have off days; a background check on how they are doing.
- On small goals (four notes at a time): "a great way to do it"; let the AI choose where in the song to start, since the first part may not be the easiest.
- On the ceiling: it is not about playing perfectly, it is confidence and foundations, then go buy a piano; early learning is about building confidence.
- Asked about gamifying, "a musical Duolingo". Carlos: not a game, novelty fades around week four; small goals as the reward; jam with people at your level, not with someone very good (intimidating). She agreed.
- "A great idea, on the right track"; she would be too scared to contact an instructor, "this would be a great place to start".
