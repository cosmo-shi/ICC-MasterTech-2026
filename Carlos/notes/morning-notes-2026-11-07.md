## 4. Copyright line (given 09:22 and 09:24)

- Splitting a song into syllables or phrases does not take it out of copyright; what matters is what the product reproduces.
- A song has separate rights: melody and harmony, lyrics, and any recording; teaching from a recording touches all three (general knowledge, unverified).
- Infringement turns on a "substantial part", judged by importance rather than length (general knowledge, unverified).
  o Australian example: in 2010 the Federal Court held that "Down Under" reproduced "a substantial part of Kookaburra" through its flute part (Larrikin Music Publishing v EMI Songs Australia; Wikipedia, opened 2026-11-07; bar count not stated on the page).
- A student's own study copy has some fair-dealing cover; a product supplying phrases to many students does not (general knowledge, unverified).
  o A school's music licence covers the school's own copying, not a vendor's app (unverified; check with the Australian Copyright Council before any file claims it).
- The way out is the design already chosen: like a tuner, the coach analyses the playing and reproduces nothing of the work.

| The coach may | The coach must never |
|---|---|
| Save a goal by title ("He's a Pirate, on piano") | Show the notes, tabs or a simplified version of a tune |
| Say what skills a piece needs (steady pulse, a repeating left hand, a minor scale) | Play the melody back |
| Ship its own drill phrases in the same key and feel | Keep any copyrighted song as built-in content |
| Plan days: pulse, left hand, both hands, bring your own sheet music | Produce an arrangement (a derivative work; also a banned topic in session-rules.md) |
| Listen to the learner's playing and give one fix | Send a learner's clip of a copyrighted tune anywhere public |

- Grey spot: a take of a real tune stored on the laptop contains the melody; keep it local, send it only to the teacher, and for copyrighted repertoire keep the card to timing and wrong-key data.
- Stage rule: public-domain or team-written phrases only; the Pirates example is for the desk, where it explains the principle in one breath.
- "Which skills does this song need" is a fit for the one saved, labelled model call, with the model told to return skills and a plan and no notes.

## 5. Plain-words coaching (given 09:28)

- The pulse, in Year 5 words: the steady beat you tap your foot to; tap it, count 1 2 3 4, play one key on each tap; it never speeds up or slows down.
- Rule: every word the coach uses gets a one-breath plain answer plus a ten-second task; a small glossary in the rules engine, not a model call.
- Vocabulary follows the age band: "beat you tap your foot to", not "tempo" or "metre"; the teacher's cue book checks the wording.
- The "what is that?" moment is a stage beat: the learner asks, the coach answers in plain words and turns it into the next tiny task.

## 7. Platform and UI (discussed 09:48)

- Concept: phone-first, for accessibility; the phone has the microphone and is what a learner's family already owns.
- Prototype: a responsive web page shown on a phone, installed to the home screen later as a PWA; no app store, no native build (stack-review-v1 s1 single file; build-plan-v1 s4 PWA deferred).
- Why not native now.
  o App stores add under-13 rules, consent flows and review time; the web keeps "nothing leaves the device" true and schools can open it on managed iPads or Chromebooks.
  o A native wrapper later (for example Capacitor) is a packaging step, not a rebuild (unverified).
- Stage stays the laptop with the USB MIDI keyboard; phones use the microphone path (tech-voice-path-proof) or on-screen keys; Web MIDI on iPhone Safari is unverified.
- UI rule: a practice session, not a conversation; no message bubbles, no typing indicator, no text box, no assistant persona.
- The screen, top to bottom.
  o The goal in the learner's words and today's step ("steady pulse", day 1 of 5).
  o The beat strip: beats as ticks, the learner's notes as dots landing on or off them; take 1 and the retake as two rows, so the change is visible at a glance.
  o One big button ("Play it", count-in); after the take, one card with the fix in big plain words, a "What does that mean?" link that opens a ten-second task, and "Try again".
  o After the third miss on one cause the card becomes "Let's ask a person", with the retake card preview and a consented "Send to your teacher".
  o A small line: "Nobody sees this unless you send it."
- Visual tone: calm, large type, one accent colour for the fix, dated journal cards for progress, no streaks or leaderboards.
- Free-text questions from children stay out: a fixed glossary behind "What does that mean?"; a model answer only in the pilot with educator approval (child-safety and moderation burden, and it is what makes an app look like a chatbot).
- True to the build: the live loop is rules, the model is one saved planning call; the UI should show that truth.

## 8. Live model compromise (discussed 10:00): rules decide, the model explains

- The live model's strength is adapting an explanation to how a learner asks; its weakness is measurement and safety; split the job on that line.
- The rules engine measures the take and picks the one cause; the model never decides what is wrong.
- The learner asks in their own words, typed or spoken; a rules router sends pain, tiredness and technique to the hand-off before the model sees them.
- The model gets a tight packet (measured cause and numbers, goal, age band, the question, the teacher's cue book and vocabulary) and returns one sentence and one task in the same fix card.
- A validator checks length, plain words, no notes or lyrics, no technique or health advice; a failure falls back to the glossary line; every exchange is logged for the educator.
- Under 15: typed text and measured numbers only, never audio, cloud off by default, consent to switch on (model-slot-options-v0 rule); older learners with consent get a looser frame.
- Stage: the saved, labelled call; desk: live if the venue network allows, with "live" on screen and the saved answers as fallback.
- Lines that work get promoted by the teacher into the cue book; the model grows the rules.

| The model does | The rules do |
|---|---|
| Reword the fix for this learner | Measure the take and choose the fix |
| Answer "what does that mean" in their words | Route pain, tiredness and technique to a person |
| Turn a vague question into the right next task | Enforce vocabulary, length and banned content |
| Draft replies the mentor approves in the pilot | Decide when coaching stops and a human takes over |

## 9. What changes per prompt (discussed 10:03)

- The rules are fixed between releases (cue rules, on-beat window, pass rule, safety router, vocabulary); a teacher or a release changes them, never a prompt.
- The learner state changes with every take and question: goal and plan progress, causes missed and how often, words tapped, phrasing, "got it" marks; on the device, on the pilot server with consent.
- The model never changes; its packet is built fresh from the state, the rule output and the question, so answers adapt because the context is new.
- Flow: take or question; rules measure, pick the cause and route; state update; packet; model; validator; fix card; "got it?" feeds the state.
- Adaptation accumulates per learner (state), per program (promoted lines at the next release) and per plan (the planning call reruns after a third miss on one cause).
- Prototype: learner state as a small JSON beside the take data (take-data-contract.md); the packet is a template; the stage swaps live for saved.
