## 1. Bottom line
- The social layer to propose (one sentence):
  o "Practice starts individual, without an audience; the social part is people: a consented share with a supervised mentor, an opt-out ensemble activity in the outreach session, and later group-level patterns and level-matched partners; no leaderboards, no streaks."
- The fear line for the pitch (23 words; ledger F146, F53, F143, C10; idea-bank D9):
  o "Too shy for a teacher? Practise without an audience; audio stays on your device by default, and a mentor joins when you choose."
- The one risk:
  o No study shows that private app practice lowers fear or brings shy beginners to a teacher; we can say it is designed for that, and test it, not that it works.
- Why the evidence supports this order:
  o Adolescent learners with high anxiety report more fear when an audience is present and when playing solo [1] (opened).
  o Lesson format alone does not decide achievement; results flip with age in a small study [2] (opened).
  o Social elements (relatedness) are the largest gamification lever, but leaderboards can hurt low performers [team file gamification-research.md; 11].
  o Regulators class streaks and reward loops as features to switch off by default for minors [9][10] (snippet).

| E5 | Teachers' best tools: low-intensity practice performances build self-efficacy when students succeed; normalise mistakes; preparation builds confidence through mastery | [3] | A private, low-stakes rehearsal before a human hears you fits the teachers' own method | The review does not test private practice or apps | opened |

- Read together:
  o Fear is about being heard and judged, and it is felt in front of the teacher, not only on stage [1][3][4].
  o Teachers build confidence with low-stakes runs that succeed [3]; a private take, one fix and a retake that works is the same shape.
  o The ledger's C7 step (learner says what changed before the score shows) adds a mastery experience the learner owns.
  o Gap: nobody has shown that private app practice raises later help-seeking. The nine-student test or pilot must measure it.

## 3. Evidence: individual versus group learning for beginners
| # | Claim | Source | What it supports | What it does not | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| G1 | 45 beginners aged 5 to 7, 24 weekly piano lessons: no overall difference group vs private; 5-year-olds did better in groups, 6 and 7 year olds did better privately | Chiu 2017, University of Washington thesis [2] | Format interacts with age; the author names growing self-consciousness as one reason | Small; not our age band | opened |
| G2 | Year 7 beginners: individual instruction beat group on performance achievement | McCarthy thesis, Michigan State [14] | Older beginners may need individual feedback | Old; one site; abstract only | snippet |
| G3 | Advanced music students practise with peers weekly (54%); 48% rate peer discussion highly beneficial; peer and teacher influence correlate (r = 0.31) | Nielsen, Johansen, Jorgensen 2018, Frontiers [15] | Peers add value alongside the teacher, not instead | University students; no effect on outcomes measured; authors warn competitive settings make peer help feel threatening | opened |
| G4 | Peer-directed, self-chosen-music learning helped bring disaffected school students into music-making | Lucy Green's informal learning work, via a 2015 book review [16] | Group learning on the learner's own song suits the two-track goal | Review of a book, not a trial | snippet |
| G5 | A 2024 scoping review maps 28 empirical studies of informal music learning (2006 to 2023) | Cambridge, British Journal of Music Education [17] | A body of work exists to cite | We did not read its findings | snippet |
| G6 | Teacher review: peer support helps with performance anxiety | [3] citing Tahirbegi 2019 | Peers can lower fear when supportive | One cited study | opened |
| G7 | Relatedness elements: g = 1.776; competence elements (badges, leaderboards): g = .277 | team file gamification-research.md (verified there) | Social connection is the strongest lever | Meta-analysis of gamified learning, not music, not children | team file |
| G8 | Relative scoring raised effort for top performers and cut it for bottom performers | Coding-contest study via search [11] | Leaderboards widen gaps; beginners sit at the bottom | One study; adults; source attribution unconfirmed | snippet |
| G9 | Near-peer: university students mentor school classes in STEM for 10+ weekly visits; in-class and online modes; 87,594 students since 2004; 264 placements in 2025; ACER evaluation 2017 | In2science (Victoria) [18] | Australian universities already run supervised near-peer mentoring at scale | Outcome figures not on the page; STEM not music | opened |
| G10 | Online e-mentoring for Year 8 and 9 students in two regional or remote schools (UNSW ASPIRE, 26 students): clear post-school pathway from 35% to 53% | NCSEHE "Click-on Mentors" summary [19] | A near-peer, online model already reaches regional Year 8 to 9 | Tiny pilot; page returned 404 on fetch | snippet |

- Read together:
  o No study says group beats individual, or the reverse, for every beginner; age and self-consciousness change the answer [2][14].
  o For Year 7 to 9, the safe design is individual practice first, then social contact through a supervised person [2][3][18].
  o The social lever is real (G7) but competitive forms hurt the weakest (G8), who are exactly our learners.

- Lean in one line: rank 1 on stage (mock, labelled), rank 2 on the vision slide, 3 and 4 as proposed future, 5 and 6 never.
- Build readiness is not child safety: a demo card does not make mentor contact safe.
- Why not buddy first: it is the most social but has the highest child-safety cost and no build by Wednesday.
- Safeguards rank 1 needs before any pilot (Codex critique, accepted; none exists in team files yet):
  o Who may mentor, who supervises, which checks and training, reply time and reply rules.
  o Contact only in a university-controlled, logged channel; no private messages, no off-platform links.
  o A route for a child or guardian to report a concern; someone reviews replies.
  o Settle "named teacher" (privacy-design-v1) versus "university-student mentor" (pitch-v2) wording.
  o Patterns: minimum cohort size, suppress rare slices, test whether a teacher can infer a child.

## 5. The spoken answers
### 5a. Jeff: "individual or group learning?" (30 s, 71 words)
- "Both, in that order. Practice starts individual: a beginner can make mistakes without an audience. Then it connects to people in the outreach programme. When stuck, the learner can share a take with a supervised mentor, with consent. In the in-person session, the teacher runs a group activity everyone plays together, opt-out. We propose AI summaries of group practice patterns to plan that session; not built yet. No leaderboards for children."

### 5b. Q and A entry (qa-prep-v1 shape; proposed Q46)
- Q46. Does this isolate learners? How does it connect individual and group learning?
- Spoken version (56 words): "Only if private practice were the whole programme. Practice starts alone; when stuck, the learner can share a take with a supervised mentor, with consent; the outreach session adds an opt-out ensemble activity. Group patterns for the teacher are a proposal, with small-group privacy checks. We have not shown this raises confidence; the pilot measures it."
- Evidence: anxious adolescents fear audiences and solo exposure (Papageorgi 2021, ages 12 to 19, opened); format results are mixed and shift with age in young children (Chiu 2017, ages 5 to 7, opened; not our band); peer and teacher influence work together (Nielsen 2018, university students, opened); EU and UK guidance on streaks and reward loops for minors (snippet).
- Honest limit: no test yet that private practice brings shy learners to a mentor; the pilot measures hand-offs.
