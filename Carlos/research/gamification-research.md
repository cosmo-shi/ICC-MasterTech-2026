# Gamification evidence file (for and against Questline)

- Purpose: the evidence behind and against gamification, so Friday's pick is deliberate, not a hunch.
- Date: 5 Nov 2026. Extends research-bank.md; does not rewrite gamified-journey-concept.md.
- Citations: [n] points to section 10. "(unverified)" means the primary page was not opened or readable.

## 1. Evidence for and against gamification in learning

### 1.1 Meta-analyses and reviews
| Source | Scope | Effect (Hedges g) | What it means for us |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sailer and Homner 2020 [1] | k = 19 cognitive, 16 motivational, 9 behavioural | Cognitive .49, motivational .36, behavioural .25 | Small effects; only cognitive holds in high-rigour studies (g = .42) |
| Huang et al. 2020 [2] | 30 studies, N = 3,083 | .464 [.244, .684] | Small to medium learning gain |
| Bai, Hew and Huang 2020 [3] | 30 interventions, N = 3,202 | .504 [.284, .723] | Medium; element type and count made no difference |
| Bolat and Tas 2023 [13] | 17 studies, 23 effects, N = 2,836, quiz tools | .62 | Larger in higher education than K-12 |
| Li, Ma and Shi 2023 [6] | 41 studies, N over 5,071 | .822, I-squared about 94% | Large but very mixed; its HE figure contradicts itself |
| Zeng et al. 2024 [7] | 22 experiments, 2008 to 2023 | .782 | Few studies; authors call it moderate |
| Li, Hew and Du 2024 [8] | 35 interventions, N = 2,500 | Intrinsic motivation .257 | Need-by-need results in 1.2 |
| Hamari, Koivisto and Sarsa 2014 [4] | Review of empirical studies | Mostly positive | Effects depend heavily on context and users |

- The 2020 syntheses agree: learning effects sit at g = .46 to .50 [1][2][3].
  o Newer, larger figures (.78 to .82) rest on fewer or more mixed studies [6][7].
  o The honest pitch claim is "small to medium", never "large".
- Rigour matters: motivational and behavioural effects lose significance in rigorous studies [1].
- Game fiction, and competition combined with collaboration, helped behavioural outcomes most [1].
- Students liked enthusiasm, feedback, recognition and goal setting [3].
  o They disliked anxiety, jealousy and elements that added no utility [3].

### 1.2 Self-determination theory applied
| Need | Gamification effect (Li, Hew and Du 2024) [8] | Elements that moved it (Sailer et al. 2017) [10] |
|---|---|---|
| Relatedness | g = 1.776, large | Avatars, meaningful stories, teammates |
| Autonomy | g = .638 | Designed decision freedom did not move it |
| Competence | g = .277, minimal | Badges, leaderboards, performance graphs |
| Intrinsic motivation | g = .257, small | Specific elements have specific effects |

- Main failure causes in gamified classes: low perceived competence and low perceived autonomy [8].
- Games hold players through autonomy, competence and relatedness, not fun alone [62].
- Implication: the social layer is the strongest lever; competence needs deliberate design.

### 1.3 Where gamification goes wrong
| Problem | Evidence | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Reward crowd-out (overjustification) | Expected rewards undermined children's interest (1973) [65]; 128-study meta-analysis (1999) [64] | Strong in lab; effect sizes not re-checked (unverified) |
| Badges plus leaderboard over a semester | Lower intrinsic motivation, satisfaction, empowerment and final exam scores [19] | One course, about 71 students (abstract unverified) |
| Points reward quantity, not quality | Points, levels, leaderboards raised output, not intrinsic motivation [63] | Lab task (unverified, PDF unreadable) |
| Rank effects | High ranks drifted to easy points; low ranks' effort faded by round 5 [18] | N = 111; neither group intrinsically motivated |
| Novelty wear-off | Effect fell after week 4 for 2 to 6 weeks, recovered weeks 6 to 10 [9] | N = 756, 14 weeks, CS1 courses |
| Narrative cost | Fiction raised satisfaction (d = .65), lowered procedural knowledge (d = -.40) [51] | One training study |

- Leaderboard evidence is mixed; who sees ranks and how often drives the outcome [18].
- Deterding's definition, the field's standard [5]:

> "the use of game design elements in non-game contexts"

- Pointsification: Robertson (2010) on adding points and badges and calling it a game [11]:

> "They are the least important bit of a game"

- Gameful design changes the course itself, not just its scoring; GradeCraft is the HE example [43].

### 1.4 Kahoot and Quizizz
- Wang and Tahir 2020 reviewed 93 Kahoot studies [12].
  o Positive for learning, class dynamics and anxiety; some studies show little or no effect [12].
  o Lead author is linked to Kahoot's origin (unverified); read as friendly evidence.
- Quiz-tool meta-analysis: g = .62, larger in higher education, Quizizz strongest [13].
- Both are session games for in-class recall; neither builds a semester-long journey.

### 1.5 Streaks, loss aversion and the dark-pattern debate
| Claim | Source | Caveat |
|---|---|---|
| 7-day streak learners 3.6 times more likely to complete a course | Duolingo blog 2022 [14] | Company data, no method, correlational |
| Streak freeze +0.38% daily actives; milestone animation +1.7% day-7 retention | Duolingo [14] | Engagement, not learning |
| Intact streaks raise repeat behaviour; breaks cut it, more with self-blame | 7 studies, JCR 2023 [15] | A repair option softens the drop |
| Nearly all of 859 streak designs count days, not learning | Mobbin design review [17] | Review, not a trial |
| Lower daily bar kept 40% more on a 7-day streak | Duolingo, via Mobbin [17] | Secondary report (unverified) |
| Streaks can turn into compulsion driven by guilt or anxiety | Smashing Magazine 2026 [16] | Practitioner commentary |

- Duolingo names loss aversion as an engine of its streak [14].
- Dark game patterns: temporal, monetary and social-capital designs that work against players [55].
- Prodigy: 22 groups told the US FTC it showed up to four times more ads than maths [33].
- Takeaway: streaks and leagues optimise opening the app; learning needs a different counter.

## 2. Habit formation: what a study loop should drive
| Mechanism | Evidence | Number |
|---|---|---|
| Habit automaticity | Lally et al. 2010, one daily behaviour, fixed cue [20] | 66 days to plateau; range 18 to 254 (predicted); 82 completers |
| Missing a day | Same study [20] | One miss had little effect; many misses lowered the plateau |
| Critique | Only 39 of 82 fitted well; measured automaticity, not habit [21] | Treat 66 days as rough |
| Implementation intentions | Gollwitzer and Sheeran 2006, 94 tests [22] | d = .65 on goal attainment |
| Practice testing, distributed practice | Dunlosky et al. 2013 [23] | High utility; rereading and underlining low |
| Retrieval practice | Roediger and Karpicke 2006 [24] | One week later: 61% recall tested vs 40% restudied |

- A 12-week unit is 84 days, longer than the 66-day figure; a unit can carry a habit [20].
- Restudy wins at five minutes, testing wins at one week [24].
  o Restudiers also felt more confident; the loop must reward delayed recall, not instant comfort [24].
- "If it is Tuesday after my tutorial, I do one quest" is an implementation intention [22].

| A good loop reinforces | A bad loop reinforces |
|---|---|
| Recall of the week's outcome, spaced over weeks [23][24] | Opening the app daily, whatever is done [17] |
| A fixed cue: same time, place or trigger [20][22] | Fear of losing a counter [14][16] |
| Competence shown against the student's own past [8][10] | Rank against classmates [18][19] |
| Recovery after a miss, without shame [15][20] | Reset to zero and self-blame [15] |
| Working with peers on the same gap [8] | Grinding for points [63][55] |

## 5. Journey, not task: design ideas and five rules
| Idea | What the evidence says | Use in Questline |
|---|---|---|
| Flow (Csikszentmihalyi) | Challenge predicted learning directly and via engagement; immersion did not [60] | Match quest difficulty to skill |
| Bartle player types | Four types drawn from MUD players, 1996 [53] | Vocabulary only, not a learning model |
| Octalysis | Often simplified, can overwhelm; no validation reported [54] | Do not cite as evidence |
| Hero's Journey | Module-design frameworks only; no outcome study found (unverified) | A skin, not a mechanism |
| Peak-end rule | Memory weighted to the peak and the end; duration neglected [52] | Design the week 12 ending |
| Onboarding to endgame | Novelty dips after week 4; familiarity recovers weeks 6 to 10 [9] | Plan for the mid-semester dip |
| Goal gradient | Effort speeds up near a goal; pre-filled stamps sped completion [61] | Show how close the next region is |

- Rule 1: make the path visible and the next step feel near.
  o Performance graphs raise competence [10]; a head start speeds completion and retention [61].
- Rule 2: keep challenge just ahead of skill.
  o Challenge was the strongest learning predictor [60]; difficulty can be estimated per learner [30].
- Rule 3: give real choices and show their consequence.
  o Missing autonomy is a main failure point [8]; GradeCraft lets students plan their path [43].
- Rule 4: play together, not against each other.
  o Relatedness g = 1.776 [8]; teammates and stories raise relatedness [10]; collaboration helps [1].
- Rule 5: end on a high and let the student keep the story.
  o Endings dominate memory [52]; narrative lifts reactions but must not replace practice [51].

## 6. Risks and mitigations
- The habit remark in transcript 06 (5 Nov follow-up; header says team-only):

> "It doesn't have to be anything, you know, addictive, but it, you know, reinforces."

- Reading: reinforce the learning behaviour; never exploit fear, scarcity or compulsion [55][16].

| Risk | Evidence | Mitigation in the literature | Questline guard |
|---|---|---|---|
| Extrinsic reward collapse | Badges and leaderboard lowered motivation and exam scores [19]; overjustification [64][65] | Informational feedback on competence [8][10] | No points currency; progress shown as outcomes held |
| Addiction, dark patterns | Temporal, monetary, social pressure [55]; streak compulsion [16]; Prodigy upsell [33] | Repairable streaks, low daily bar [15][17] | No daily streak, no pay-to-skip, no random rewards |
| Equity, low performers | Rank effects fade and turn extrinsic [18]; anxiety, jealousy [3] | Anonymised comparison [44] | No ranks; compare with your own week 1 |
| Accessibility | WCAG 2.2.1: users can turn off, adjust or extend time limits [56] | Untimed by default [56] | No countdowns; plain skin default |
| Behavioural data privacy | Privacy named a main gap in AI plus gamification [50]; OAIC row in research-bank.md | Minimise data, host on campus | Private map; cohort view with minimum group size |
| Teacher burden | Quests built by hand [26]; Level Up rules need setup [31]; staff tuned content [44] | AI drafts, human approves | Coordinator approves a generated map; ten-minute target untested |
| Novelty decay | Dip from week 4, recovery weeks 6 to 10 [9] | Longer interventions show larger effects [6] | Pilot runs past week 10; measure late |
| Childish or costly narrative | Fiction lowered procedural knowledge (d = -.40) [51] | Light, optional narrative [51] | Professional skin; story off switch |

### Why can't I just use Claude?
- A student can paste an outline into Claude and get a study plan; the solo map is not structural.
- Structural: twelve weeks of state for 450 students, an approved map, parties matched across a cohort.
- Evidence: relatedness is gamification's largest effect (g = 1.776), and a chatbot is single-player [8].

### Pros and cons
| Pros | Cons |
|---|---|
| Answers the question literally: the unit becomes a journey | Learning effects are small to medium and fragile [1] |
| Social layer hits the strongest motivational lever [8] | Novelty dips after week 4 and can sink a short pilot [9] |
| No content rewrite; any unit, any discipline | Badges and ranks in a semester course have backfired [19] |
| Quests can ride the best-evidenced study techniques [23][24] | Judges may see a gamified LMS, which already exists [31] |
| Precedents show demand, and no AI incumbent was found [26][43] | Quest quality depends on the outline and generated items; unproven |
| A 12-week unit fits the habit window [20] | A music merge enters an occupied consumer space [57][59] |

## 9. What to keep, drop and add in Questline
- Keep the honest claim: learning effects g = .46 to .50; only cognitive gains survive rigour [1][2][3].
- Keep "progress, not points": badges plus a leaderboard cut motivation and exam scores [19][18].
- Keep parties and raids as the core: relatedness is the largest effect, g = 1.776 [8][1].
- Keep the story optional, plain by default: narrative lifted satisfaction but cut procedural learning [51].
- Add: every side quest is spaced retrieval practice; 61% vs 40% recall at one week [24][23].
- Add: build competence with adaptive difficulty; competence is the weakest effect, g = .277 [8][30].
- Add: weekly "when and where" plan cards, no daily streak; d = .65, one miss barely matters [22][20].
- Add: let students repair a missed week; unrepairable broken streaks cut engagement [15].
- Add: run the pilot past week 10 and measure late; effects dip from week 4 [9].
- Drop "nobody does this": Classcraft Quests and GradeCraft came first; claim AI generation plus cohort [26][43].
