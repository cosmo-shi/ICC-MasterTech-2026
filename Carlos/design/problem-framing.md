# Problem framing - MasterTech Challenge 2026

- Challenge: "How might we use AI and emerging technology to transform the way students learn and succeed?"
- Draft 5 Nov 2026, revised for the mentor's steer. Scale anchor: one first-year health unit with 450 students.
- Answer test: every idea must visibly change how a student learns and succeeds in the demo.

## The picture first (6 Nov, after Ivan and Dan; numbers verified in research-bank.md, 6 Nov section)
- Rule: name one person, one moment, what goes wrong, then the number. The solution waits. Say it once without the word "AI" and check it still stands.

### Version A, the music lead: a future primary teacher
- Imagine a second-year primary-education student. In week 8 of placement she has to lead a class song.
- Her degree gave her about eight hours of music, down 53% since 2009. Music is 1% of a primary teaching degree.
- About 4% of new primary graduates from surveyed universities have any music specialisation; 72% of those universities offer none.
- She feels "I can't teach that". So the children get music one term a year, as half of NSW primary teachers report.
- An estimated two-thirds of primary schools lack quality, sequential music education. Nobody in that room can fix it but her.
- Problem in one sentence: future teachers must teach music they were never taught, with no step between "can't play" and "teach it".
- How might we give that student a first small step tonight, and a record of getting there, before the placement?

### Version B, the general unit: any first-year
- A first-year student in week 3 sees one big assessment due in week 8 and a list of readings. There is no next step.
- Students predicted 33.9 days for a thesis and took 55.5. "I'll start tomorrow" is the plan.
- By week 7 they are behind and studying alone. The content was fine; the shape of the work was the problem.
- How might we turn a big assessment into small goals a student can start tonight, so they find themselves ahead, not behind?

- Ivan's frame for the stage: music learning needs a place, an instrument and a teacher in one room. Remove one and the model breaks; the app fills what is missing and augments what is there.

## General framing (5 Nov, health unit anchor; still the fallback)

## The problem in one sentence
- A first-year student can get AI answers in seconds, yet rarely knows what use is allowed, where they are stuck, or whether they understand.
  o Even where AI is allowed, "how much" and "for what" are rarely defined.
  o At 450 students per unit, nobody can tell them one to one.

## Who feels it
| Role | Who exactly | The moment | What hurts |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary | First-year health undergraduate in a 450-student unit | 11 pm before the case study is due, AI open in another tab | Unsure what AI use is allowed; fears misconduct; gets answers instead of understanding; cannot see where they are stuck |
| Secondary | Coordinator of that 450-student unit | Week 3 lecture prep, then marking week | Cannot attend to 450 students (Rodney); sees only final answers; cannot tell learning from offloading |

- Student first: the challenge asks how students learn and succeed (mentor steer, 5 Nov).
  o The student is the hero of the demo; their hands drive it.
- Coordinator gains as a by-product of the same data, never by watching students.
  o Must never feel like surveillance; the team flagged this risk on 2 Nov.

## Why now
- Generative AI is already in students' hands, mostly outside any university guardrail.
  o 6,960 students at four Australian universities: 49% used GenAI for feedback on work (Henderson et al. 2025, research bank).
- Direction beats answers: hint-only GPT Tutor lifted practice 127% and removed the 17% exam harm seen with open GPT-4 (Bastani et al. 2025, about 1,000 students).
- Sponsors name cognitive offloading as the core risk: AI doing the thinking (Frank).
- Employers want graduates who use AI as a tool and critique its output (Chris).
- Assessment is shifting from the final product towards process and authentic work.
  o Flinders' AI Assessment Scale already asks for a prompt-and-output appendix; Sydney's two-lane model; TEQSA's three reform pathways (research bank).
- AI-writing detectors are not trusted as proof of misconduct.
  o ACU logged nearly 6,000 alleged cases in 2024, about 90% AI, then dropped the Turnitin AI detector; Sydney: detection "not possible" unsupervised (research bank).
- Australian students and staff are privacy-sensitive and resist device-level access.
  o Still a gap: no Australian survey of students' views on sharing prompt logs found. House inquiry warns against tools storing data offshore (research bank).
- Capable models can now run on campus, so student data need not leave the university.
  o Cogniti (University of Sydney) keeps student data under institutional control on Azure; on-campus models still unproven at scale (research bank).

## Design principles (apply to every direction)
| Principle | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Student is the hero | The student learns or succeeds differently, on screen, in the demo |
| Outcome Based Education (OBE) | Outcome plus demonstrated process, both mapped to unit learning outcomes |
| Thinking, not volume | Quality of questioning, follow-ups, revisions and critique of AI output; never tokens or prompt counts |
| Opt-in, assignment-scoped | Only activity inside the assignment space is recorded; the student owns it and chooses what is shared |
| No device access | No phone, laptop, screen, keystroke or browser monitoring |
| Sponsor technology fit | Show where Cisco technology plausibly fits; confirm every product claim with the Cisco rep |
| Demo-grade and simple | Realistic shortcuts allowed; one clear story that feels finished |

## What success looks like in 12 months
| Measure | Target (goal to agree with a sponsor, not data) |
|---|---|
| Pilot | One unit at one sponsor university, one full semester |
| Student use | Over half the cohort uses the tool at least weekly, voluntarily |
| Student understanding | Higher than a matched non-pilot cohort, by survey and formative checks |
| Student clarity on AI rules | Most pilot students can state what AI use is allowed on their task |
| Coordinator benefit | Stuck learning outcomes visible by week 3, from anonymous aggregates only |
| Privacy | No student data leaves university-controlled infrastructure; privacy impact assessment passed |
| Proceed | Sponsor funds expansion to three units in the next semester |

## Stakeholders (Chris, 5 Nov: map everyone, add no burden to teachers)
| Stakeholder | What they need from us |
|---|---|
| Student | Learns differently, owns their data, nothing installed, no surveillance feel |
| Unit coordinator or teacher | Zero extra burden; complements the class; cohort view only as a by-product |
| University leaders and IT | Data stays on campus, fits existing licences and identity, clear pilot cost |
| Parents and employers | Graduates who think critically and can critique AI output |
| Regulators (TEQSA, OAIC) | Assessment integrity by design; privacy by default |
| Sponsors (Cisco, UC, Flinders, La Trobe) | A credible pilot path and a plausible fit with their technology |

## Scope
| In | Out |
|---|---|
| Higher education, one large first-year unit | K-12 and VET (possible later) |
| Written, take-home assessment tasks | Exams and proctoring |
| Student-owned, assignment-scoped learning-journey record | Device-level monitoring of phones, laptops, screens or keystrokes |
| OBE process evidence: questioning, follow-ups, revisions | Scoring by tokens, prompt counts or time spent |
| AI that guides, checks and summarises | AI detection, misconduct policing, or AI that assigns marks |
| Synthetic student data for the prototype | Real student data before ethics and privacy approval |
| Mock LMS and login screens | Building real LMS or identity integrations |
