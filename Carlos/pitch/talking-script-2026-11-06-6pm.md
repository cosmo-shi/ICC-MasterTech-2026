### Picture 0: mine (Carlos, first person; quotes as logged in evidence-carlos-singing-practice.md)
- This year I started recording vocal covers at home, alone, over karaoke videos. I am a very big introvert.
- Everyone said "just hire a vocal coach". The thought gave me anxiety, and when I tried, my performance dropped.
- So I used an AI in text to clean up the recordings. Coaching was never the plan. It crept in, because what I heard was about my singing.
- I typed what I felt. "I think I'm singing forward." "My voice is too high and not whole." "Some parts off key, no pitch guide."
- Every one of those pointed the wrong way. "Singing forward" was the recording running late. "Too high" was the song sitting above my range.
- The AI could not hear me. It had to write scripts to measure my takes afterwards, and I still could not say which version was better.
- One night, nine takes, three hours. Eleven mixes. I never picked one.
- Then one cue worked: "don't get louder, narrow the vowel." One retake. The high notes came out lighter and the pitch wobbles mostly went away. Measured, not felt.
- That is the whole product in one line: hear me, tell me one thing, show me it worked.
- And the note I still could not reach is exactly where a real teacher should have taken over. Nobody sent me.
- Simone's version: piano in a classroom was hard even with a teacher, and there was no piano at home to try things on.
- Justin's version: he played in a class of twenty and froze, afraid of a mistake in front of everyone.
- Three of us, three instruments, one gap: nobody could hear us and tell us one thing to fix, privately.
- And we had a PC, a microphone, internet and an AI. Now take all of that away.

### Picture A: the child, Year 5, a small school three hours from Canberra
- Her school has one teacher for everything. That teacher got about eight hours of music in a four-year degree.
- In a 2024 survey, half of primary teachers said music happens one term a year. For her, it is a song at assembly.
- There is no piano in the house. The nearest music teacher is an hour's drive, and both parents work shifts.
- The cheapest keyboard rental is about twelve dollars a month, but it needs a six-month contract and a card. The ACT has no library that lends one.
- The internet drops most evenings. In very remote Australia, almost one in three households has connection problems most of the time.
- She is curious. She taps rhythms on the desk. Nobody will ever know if she had talent, because nobody will ever hear her try.

### Picture B: the teenager, Year 10, a regional town
- He wants to play guitar. His cousin left one behind, missing a string.
- There is no music elective this year; the teacher who ran it left and was not replaced.
- He learns from videos. Nobody tells him his thumb is in the wrong place, so the bad habit sets in week one.
- A weekly lesson costs more than his Saturday shift pays, and the drive is forty minutes each way.
- He practises in the shed so nobody hears. After three weeks he stops, and tells himself he will start again in the holidays.
- He is not lazy and not untalented. He had no instrument that worked, no feedback, no time and no lift.

### Picture C: the young adult, first year at university, first in the family
- She came to Canberra from a regional town on a pathway programme. She always wanted to play, and assumed it was too late.
- It is not: forty per cent of adults who play continuously never played in high school.
- She shares a flat, works evenings and studies days. There is no piano, no spare hour, and no spare money for lessons.
- Singing or playing in front of strangers feels like exposure, so she never joins the campus band.
- What she needs is a private first step: ten minutes a night, a phone that listens, and a human when she is ready.

### The common thread, then the number
- Traditional music learning needs a place, an instrument and a teacher in the same room, plus money and time. Our learners are missing at least three of those.
- We felt the gap with every resource in hand. The kid in Picture A has the gap and none of the resources.
- The first step is the whole barrier. After the first step, people find a way.
- Then the number: seventy-two per cent of surveyed universities offer no primary music specialisation; none of our sponsor universities has a music school.

## 2. The idea (45 seconds)
- We are building a practice coach for music fundamentals that works on a phone, with or without an instrument.
- The learner names what they want: make this cover sound good, play this song for mum. The AI splits that into small goals, and each small goal carries one fundamental.
- They play or sing ten seconds; the AI listens and says one thing to fix; the retake shows it worked. It never generates music.
- So they improve on the thing they want, and they improve overall, without being told they are doing a course. That is where our gamification research went: structure and habit, not a game.
- The app has a ceiling. When the learner reaches it, the app hands them to a real teacher. Humans and AI together, not AI instead of a teacher.
- The learner is a young person without access: regional, rural, remote or low income. Adult beginners are welcome.
- The stakeholder is universities. They already run outreach into small rural schools; this gives that outreach a music track.
- Around the coach sits a community layer: shared instruments, a buddy, a jam match. That is secondary; the coach is the product.
