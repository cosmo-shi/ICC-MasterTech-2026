# Carmen — UX journey, personas and visual direction

Working design brief | 9 November 2026 | For the team, Miro and Claude Code

## Status and use

This is a proposed future experience and a set of provisional personas, not a report of interviews or usability tests. The named learners are fictional. Their questions, feelings, barriers and motivations are hypotheses to test. No emotion scores, student quotations, measured outcomes or commercial commitments have been invented.

This document builds on Music_App_Master_Context.md, version 2.0, and the two supplied images: the persona/journey template and the music/app moodboard. The current scope remains the entire primary suite, Foundation–Year 6, with variable complexity and meaningful secondary/older pathways. A focused journey does not narrow the product to one year band.

Use the concise tables as board content. Keep the detailed learning journey and research questions alongside them. Put this file in docs/carmen/ and ask Claude Code to consult it when implementing navigation, interaction, visual components and recovery states. It is an addendum to the existing product brief; it does not authorise cloud child-data processing, unreviewed cultural content or invented AI capabilities.

## 1. What to change in the supplied template

Use one actor and one scenario per journey. A student's experience of trying music and an institution's decision to fund a programme involve different goals, actions and handoffs. Keep separate maps, connected where a teacher introduces the activity. Journey mapping guidance supports defining the actor, scenario, phases and opportunities explicitly. [UX01]

Label this board Proposed future-state journey — assumptions to validate. An observed current-state map should be built from accounts of what learners and teachers actually do today. Do not silently turn a desirable product experience into a claim about current behaviour. GOV.UK recommends consolidating similar experiences and separating materially different journeys. [UX02]

The template's Discover / Consider / Decide / Use / Retain labels can be retained for the overview. For student-facing discussion, name them Find it / Try it / Choose a start / Make and learn / Continue. Decide means choosing an activity or support, not a child making a purchase. Expand Use into a second, detailed map so the learning experience is visible.

Add three things below the existing rows: evidence status, responsible role, and a useful success signal. Attach an observation or research question to each important assumption. This follows the DTA's emphasis on understanding context, researching needs and validating designs. The DTA standard is useful guidance here, not a claim of government certification. [UX03]

## 2. Copy for the top of the board

**Product:** Carmen — an accessible music playground and learning studio.

**Primary users:** Learners across Foundation–Year 6, especially those needing an accessible starting point without a personal instrument or regular individual tuition.

**Secondary users:** High-school and older learners using suitable musical depth, examples and presentation.

**Facilitators:** Teachers, parents/carers and learning-support staff.

**Potential customers/partners:** Schools, outreach programmes, universities and other institutions; purchaser, funding and delivery roles require validation.

**Sponsor note:** Event sponsorship and product adoption are different relationships. Record confirmed event sponsors separately. Do not present a university as a product customer, research partner or endorser without that relationship being established.

**Journey scenario:** After a teacher introduces Carmen, a learner uses an available device to try an instrument, make a short original musical idea, use or decline support, keep their work, and continue at the next planned session.

**Learner outcome:** I can make and change a musical idea, understand something about my choice, and decide what to try next.

**Map boundary:** First introduction through the next planned session. Home discovery, borrowing a device and a school purchase are separate scenarios to research.

## 3. Provisional personas

These are behavioural starting points, not age categories, diagnoses or measured population segments. A learner may need different support in different tasks. Proto-personas make assumptions explicit and should be revised through research. [UX04]

## 4. Main learner journey — content for the supplied grid

Actor: P2, Alex. Scenario: teacher-introduced first activity and the next planned session. All thoughts, feelings and pain points below are hypotheses. Opportunities are proposed design responses.

| Row | Discover — Find it | Consider — Try it | Decide — Choose a start | Use — Make and learn | Retain — Continue |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Actions | Watches a teacher demonstration; sees a peer make a musical idea. | Opens the instrument and tries a few sounds using comfortable controls. | Chooses free play or a guided activity; hears the goal and an example. | Makes a phrase, listens, compares, chooses help or revises; tries a fresh task. | Reopens saved work, hears earlier ideas and chooses a next activity or project. |
| Thoughts / questions | Can I make music too? Do I need to know the notes? | Will this work for me? Can I control the sound? | What will I make? Can I change my choice? | What changed? Does that fit my idea? What should I try? | Where is my music? What can I do differently now? |
| Possible feelings | Curiosity, uncertainty or little interest. | Enjoyment, uncertainty or frustration if the controls fail. | Readiness or overload if choices and instructions are unclear. | Concentration, frustration, discovery or pride; the experience can move between them. | Satisfaction, renewed interest or disappointment if access/work is lost. |
| Pain points to test | Unclear relevance; impression that music requires prior talent or an instrument. | Silent audio, slow response, inaccessible targets or too much setup. | Reading load, unfamiliar labels, too many choices or a mismatched task. | Generic correction, interruptions, unwanted AI changes or no route out of a mistake. | Lost work, unavailable device, repetitive activities or presentation that no longer fits. |
| Opportunities | Show a real, reachable musical result and a clear invitation to try. | Provide a playable first screen, sound check and adjustable controls. | Offer a manageable starting choice, an audible example and easy backtracking. | Prioritise one supported observation; preserve original work; let the learner direct, reject and retry. | Show save location/status; make resuming clear; offer meaningful progression without streak pressure. |

Do not draw an emotion curve as measured data. Ask learners how moments felt and retain uncertainty, disagreement and neutral responses. Facial expressions, timing or errors do not authorise AI emotion inference.

### Make the map actionable

Proposed role ownership can be assigned to actual team members later.

| Stage | Useful observation | Proposed owner | Highest-priority research question |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Discover | Learner can describe or demonstrate what they could do next. | Product/design + educator | Does the introduction communicate a worthwhile and achievable activity? |
| Consider | Learner intentionally produces, changes and stops sound; operational help is recorded separately. | Audio/interface engineer + designer | Do sound, controls or setup prevent participation? |
| Decide | Learner understands the selected goal and can change the choice. | Content/learning designer | Is the starting choice understandable without steering the learner to a preferred answer? |
| Use | Learner can compare a change, choose a response and attempt a related task with assistance recorded. | Music/AI engineer + educator | Does the interaction support a musical decision and understanding, rather than merely a polished output? |
| Retain | Work is recoverable in the intended device context; the next activity remains meaningful. | Storage/interface engineer + educator | Can the learner actually continue, and what prevents or motivates it? |

These are proposed measures, not achieved targets. Do not treat an access adjustment or planned adult mediation as failure. Separate it from unplanned help needed because the interface is confusing.

## 5. Expand Use — the core musical experience

This is the most important map for implementation. Steps can repeat, branch or be skipped when appropriate; free play remains available. These rows describe a proposed learning experience, not a rigid screen-per-step wizard.

| Moment | Learner goal/action | Carmen must support | Friction and recovery to design |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Get ready | Choose comfortable controls and establish that sound works. | An obvious sound control, readable labels, input alternatives and a quick check. | Silent or blocked audio: show a clear enable/test action; preserve the current activity. |
| Explore | Make, repeat, change and stop a sound. | Responsive sampled piano/percussion and uncluttered playable space. | Accidental actions: stop, undo or reset without penalty; restore focus after changes. |
| Choose an intention | Hear a musical example and decide what to explore. | One understandable goal, a short demonstration and a manageable choice. | Unknown vocabulary: demonstrate it in music, then offer a short explanation. |
| Make an attempt | Play or construct an original phrase and hear it back. | Preserve raw input and authored work; distinguish live playing from constructed playback. | Slow/quantised playback: do not present assistance as the learner's live accuracy. |
| Notice a change | Predict or listen to a goal-related musical response. | A real model proposes a bounded plan; verify the declared symbolic changes before playback. | AI unavailable or invalid output: retain the phrase and offer accurately labelled reviewed support. |
| Direct and revise | Accept, reject, adjust, ask for different help or keep an interpretation. | Original and partner contributions remain distinguishable; one useful feedback priority. | Too many corrections: let the learner choose a focus; provide another demonstration or teacher help. |
| Try independently | Apply the idea in a fresh related task. | Remove teaching hints and answer generation; retain access settings. | Difficulty persists: offer a practice route and record the evidence accurately, without an ability label. |
| Create, keep and stop | Develop a small piece, decide what to keep and finish comfortably. | Replay, undo, understandable saving and a clear return point. | Storage unavailable/shared device: explain the limitation and provide the agreed adult-supported route. |

The interface should use child-understandable language; model IDs, hashes and invariant diagnostics belong in engineering evidence or an appropriate teacher view. Musical analysis supports observations; it cannot establish creative intention or a diagnosis from timing alone.

### Three priority moments

1. The first intentional sound: does the child experience a clear relationship between action and result?
2. The first difficulty: can the child recover, choose support and retain control of their music?
3. The first return: can the child find their work and continue an idea?

Shared-device saving requires special attention. Saved on this device does not mean available on another device or safe from browser-data clearing. Local profiles are not secure individual isolation by themselves. Do not promise privacy or persistence that has not been implemented and verified.

## 6. Vary the experience across the entire primary suite

These are proposed entry experiences and content directions. Validate them with educators and learners; they are not complete programmes or rigid age restrictions. Musical demand, reading demand, input complexity and support should be adjustable separately.

| Pathway | Meaningful task | Interface/support emphasis | Possible evidence, limited to what is observed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Foundation | Create a short sound story using intentional sound/silence or dynamics choices. | Demonstration, a few large choices, repetition and adult mediation when useful. | An intentional contrast, accessible choice or adult observation; no assumed notation knowledge. |
| Years 1–2 | Make a rhythmic conversation and explore a rest or repeated pattern. | Hear–try–compare; short instructions and visible time structure where useful. | Listening discrimination and live timing recorded separately. |
| Years 3–4 | Develop a melodic idea and control a relationship while keeping a rhythmic pattern. | Linked sound/representation, optional hints and ownership of a musical phrase. | A controlled musical change and a fresh application. |
| Years 5–6 | Arrange motifs into a purposeful form; refine texture and explain a choice. | More layers, structural editing, comparison and documentation. | Original work, revisions, interpretation and appropriately supported explanations. |
| Secondary/older | Develop a multi-part composition or interpretation using a chosen musical constraint. | More precise controls and analysis; dignified examples and optional deeper explanations. | Advanced musical decisions and independent application; no implied certification or physical technique transfer. |

The same visual identity can serve every pathway. Adjust the density, examples, character presence and explanatory depth; do not define an older beginner as a failed younger learner. Include disabled users in the design and test process rather than assuming one access setting works for everyone. [UX05]

## 8. Institutional relationship — keep it separate from the child

| Step | Question the institution must answer | Carmen evidence to develop |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Recognise a need | Which specific group or programme has a problem worth addressing? | Interviews and contextual evidence, including existing alternatives. |
| Assess fit | How would this complement teaching or outreach? | A working musical activity, access evidence and a clear delivery model. |
| Plan a pilot | Who owns delivery, permission, support, evaluation and budget? | Agreed responsibilities, a bounded protocol and complete delivery costs. |
| Evaluate | Is the learner/teacher outcome worthwhile relative to another approach? | Feasibility and comparative evidence with stated limitations. |
| Continue or stop | Who pays, why renew, and what support is sustainable? | Actual willingness to pay, repeat use, delivery costs and an honest decision. |

A university may be an outreach partner, evaluator, funder or purchaser. Those roles are not interchangeable. The app's visual appeal, an event prize or a grant is not evidence of recurring demand.

## 9. What the moodboard's style is called

**Useful descriptor:** playful flat illustration with tactile UI.

**More precise design brief:** an illustrated music-learning interface using bold colour, rounded geometry, readable type and restrained dimensional feedback.

These are descriptive terms, not one formally defined movement. Useful reference-search terms include playful flat UI, illustrated learning app, rounded geometric illustration, tactile buttons and 2.5D interface. Character-led design is an additional choice if original characters serve the product.

In the supplied moodboard, the lower app references share bright colour blocks, generous rounded forms, expressive headings, simple illustrations and controls that look approachable. The lower-right phone is shown in perspective with some depth. It demonstrates that a little layering can create tactility without requiring a glossy rendered visual world. This is an interpretation of the supplied image, not an identification of the apps or their authors.

Some bold outlines overlap with neo-brutalist references, but neo-brutalism is an incomplete main brief for this board. Rounded, restrained depth is also different from a full claymorphic interface. Naming either trend alone would leave too much of Carmen's intended clarity, musical purpose and age flexibility unspecified.

Duolingo documents a playful, minimal vector illustration system and clear, recognisable shapes. That provides a reference for consistency and clarity, not evidence that copying the brand or adding characters improves Carmen's learning outcomes. [VD01]

## 11. Carmen's proposed identity — Sound Workshop

**Direction:** an inviting illustrated music workshop where learners make, hear, change and keep musical ideas.

Keep the established name Carmen and the existing Sound Workshop direction. This is a working identity recommendation, not a claim of exclusive ownership of a visual style.

| Element | Carmen direction | Implementation boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shape language | Rounded keycaps, drum circles, note-like ovals and clear phrase blocks; a small repeated set of forms. | Keep recognisable controls and correct notation. Avoid turning every object into a different decorative blob. |
| Colour | Retain the existing warm canvas #FFF8E8, dark ink #2B2341 and cobalt #3658D4. Consider a warm yellow accent after contrast checks. | Assign colours to roles, keep the instrument readable and pair meaning with labels/shapes. A palette alone is not accessibility validation. |
| Typography | Retain Grandstander for brief expressive headings and Atkinson Hyperlegible Next for reading/controls, subject to actual rendering and licence verification. | Make instructions and notation easy to read. A typeface name is not evidence of universal accessibility. |
| Illustration | Original 2D vector scenes or symbols with simple silhouettes and musical purpose. | Do not copy Duo, its character proportions, distinctive assets, lesson-path styling or overall screen compositions. |
| Depth | A small consistent button edge or pressed state to show what can be used. | Keep the playable surface clear; decorative 3D assets are not a requirement. |
| Motion | A key depresses, a beat marker advances, a phrase change becomes visible. | Motion should explain an action; allow reduced motion, and avoid decorative loops while the learner listens or performs. |
| Sound | Real sampled instrument sound and intentional, controllable interface cues. | Effects and celebrations must not mask the musical task or interrupt a phrase. |
| Characters | Optional, original and musically useful; a facilitator rather than the centre of every screen. | Younger and older learners should be able to use a dignified interface without constant character commentary. |
| Navigation | Clear routes to play, guided learning and saved work; one coherent music workspace for the main task. | Validate labels and information architecture; do not add unrelated cards just to resemble an app dashboard. |
| Feedback | Show what the learner did, a useful comparison and a choice of next action. | Avoid an omniscient conversational persona, generic praise or aesthetic grading of original work. |

Aim for consistency in drawing, type, spacing, language, sound and behaviour. Even attractive individual illustrations will look generic if they come from unrelated visual systems.

### Connect visual design to the journey

| Journey moment | Visual/interaction job |
| --- | --- |
| Discover | A reachable musical outcome and a clear invitation to try. |
| Try | An obvious instrument and responsive pressed states. |
| Choose | A small number of understandable options with audible examples. |
| Learn/create | Quiet space around the instrument; clear relationship between sound, representation and the chosen change. |
| Continue | Recognisable saved pieces and a clear way to resume or explore. |

Use photography from the moodboard for context, outreach or selected authentic stories where licensed and appropriate. Establish one consistent illustrated vocabulary for the main app. Avoid mixing low-light concert photography, unrelated cartoon packs and glossy renders across routine lesson screens.

### Brief to give Claude Code

Use the Sound Workshop direction: a playful illustrated music studio, with 2D vector artwork, bold but controlled colour, rounded musical geometry, readable controls and restrained tactile button feedback. Preserve Carmen's existing warm/cobalt/ink palette and type roles unless a tested change is recorded. Let instruments and student-created music occupy the main workspace. Use motion to clarify musical actions and respect reduced-motion preferences. Give each primary pathway appropriate content and support, while older learners receive dignified presentation and real musical depth. Follow the supplied references for qualities such as clarity and warmth; create original assets and compositions. Implement feedback, recovery and saved-work states as carefully as the first screen.

## 13. Research and validation plan

Start with the most consequential assumptions, not whether participants like the colours. GOV.UK describes small, repeated qualitative rounds, commonly 4–8 participants for the relevant method. That is not a universal minimum, a representative sample or an efficacy-study design. Plan multiple focused rounds to cover the primary bands, support needs and older learners. [UX07]

Involve children directly as well as teachers/carers, using suitable participation methods and school/carer permission and child assent. Provide accessible ways to respond and to stop. UNICEF's participatory-design guidance supports involving children and young people as contributors. [UX08]

### Research in four steps

1. **Learn the current experience.** Ask learners and teachers about the last real occasion they tried to make or teach music. Observe available devices, sound conditions, control needs and work-saving routines. Record alternatives already in use.
2. **Test the route.** Try a low-fidelity prototype or working flow. Observe starting, choosing, playing, recovering, saving and resuming. Compare wording and layout only when a concrete question justifies it.
3. **Test the musical interaction.** Use actual learner material; observe comparison, rejection, revision and a related independent task. Compare learned planning with reviewed rules when the feature works. A polished assisted result does not prove understanding.
4. **Test continued use.** Return in a later session. Investigate whether work can be resumed, what the learner remembers, whether the next activity fits and what work the teacher must do.

Different methods answer different questions. A first-session usability check cannot establish delayed learning, institutional demand or benefits across all children. The DTA recommends combining methods such as open-ended interviews and observed tasks. [UX03]

### Neutral tasks to use

| Question | Task/prompt | What to record |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Is the first action obvious? | Show me how you would make a sound. | First action, successful intentional control, unplanned help and audio problems. |
| Can the learner form a goal? | Choose something you would like to try. Tell or show me what you expect. | Choice, interpretation and points of uncertainty; do not supply the intended answer. |
| Is the response understandable? | What do you notice between these versions? | Their comparison, what evidence it supports and where the explanation is unclear. |
| Is creative control real? | You can change this music however you want. | Whether they can revise or reject the partner's material and retain their own work. |
| Can a mistake be recovered from? | Observe a real difficulty; avoid manufacturing distress. | Recovery, support choice and whether progress is lost. |
| Can work be resumed? | At the next session, ask them to find their earlier idea. | Device/profile conditions, successful recovery and adult support. |
| Can the teacher act? | Ask the teacher to decide who might need a check-in and why. | Evidence used, uncertainty, interpretation and preparation/support burden. |

Use optional self-report for feelings and confidence. Do not equate quietness, smiling, speed or a correct assisted answer with enjoyment, motivation or learning.

### Success signals

- **Access:** Intentional participation using suitable controls and support, with blockers identified.
- **Usability:** Goals and controls understood; recovery and resuming work succeed in the intended environment.
- **Musical understanding:** A fresh, appropriate task supports a specific claim; teaching support and access adjustments are recorded separately.
- **Agency:** Learners can choose, decline, undo, revise and stop; the original work remains theirs.
- **Teacher value:** Evidence helps a real intervention or plan with manageable preparation and interpretation.
- **Sustainability:** The activity works on intended devices, with maintainable assets, content and support costs.

Set quantitative thresholds after a baseline and an agreed task definition. Do not substitute session length, streaks, clicks, decorative engagement or a mood score for educational value. No analytics upload is authorised by this document; use minimised research records and the existing local-data rules.

## 14. Implementation priorities from the map

**First:** sound readiness, playable controls, one clear activity route, useful feedback, recovery, original-work preservation and honest saving/resuming. These underpin every primary pathway.

**Alongside:** meaningful tasks across all four primary bands, accessible input/representation options, educator review and a modest teacher evidence view.

**After the foundation works:** deepen the catalogue and older pathways; add illustration variants and optional interactions where evidence supports them.

Treat a new animation, character or camera feature as a proposal with a purpose. It must improve a task or experience enough to justify its implementation and maintenance. A distinctive visual identity is valuable, but it does not by itself establish Carmen's technical innovation, teaching effectiveness or competitive advantage.
